BUSINESS REPORT: INDIAN E-COMMERCE PRICING & REVENUE GROWTH ANALYSIS REPORT
Period: January 2023 - December 2025 (36 months) | Scope: 30,600 orders across 14 states, 8 categories Prepared by: Raushan

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 

Over the 36-month period analyzed, the business generated ₹217.45 crore in revenue across 970,188 units sold, 
but topline growth was essentially flat — a ~1% CAGR from 2023 to 2025 (+0.2% in Year 2, +1.8% in Year 3).
This stagnation is not uniform: it masks a 23-percentage-point swing between the best-performing state (Kerala, +11.9%) and the worst (Tamil Nadu, -11.6%),
and a similar divergence across categories. The data also surfaces a pricing problem — the average discount across all orders is 38.1%,
yet discount depth has almost no relationship with revenue (correlation ≈ 0) while it does drive unit volume (correlation +0.39)
The business is discounting for volume, not profit, and a 20-40% discount band consistently outperforms deeper discounting on revenue per order. 
The recommendations below focus on reallocating growth investment toward proven states/categories,
correcting the discounting strategy, and protecting margin in high-competition, high-inventory-pressure conditions.

KEY FINDINGS 
1. Flat topline growth. Total revenue grew from ₹71.96cr (2023) to ₹72.10cr (2024) to ₹73.39cr (2025) — a compound annual growth rate of ~1%.
2. Revenue is regionally concentrated and diverging. 6 of 14 states grew double-digit or high single-digit (Kerala +11.9%, Haryana +11.5%, Gujarat +8.8%,
   Maharashtra +8.0%), while 3 states declined meaningfully (Tamil Nadu -11.6%, Odisha -6.6%, Madhya Pradesh -6.5%).
3. Category portfolio is top-heavy. Premium Lifestyle (29.8%) and Electronics (23.9%) together account for 53.7% of all revenue;
   Grocery Essentials contributes just 1.4% and is the fastest-declining category (-11.2%).
4. Discounting does not buy revenue. Correlation between discount % and revenue is -0.01 (effectively none), while correlation between discount % and units sold is
   +0.39. The 20-40% discount band produces the highest average revenue per order (₹74,214); the 60%+ band produces the lowest (₹63,950) despite selling the most units.
5. Festival events work, and work unevenly. Festival-tagged orders (25.2% of all orders) generate 60% higher average revenue per order than normal-period orders.
   The lift ranges from +65.4% (Beauty & Personal Care) down to +46.1% (Fashion).
6. Discounting harder under pressure doesn't pay off. In high-competition conditions, average discount rises to 42.9% but average revenue per order (₹70,922) is still
   lower than in low-competition conditions (₹72,217 at just 33.8% discount). The same pattern holds for high inventory pressure (43.2% discount, ₹70,217 revenue) vs.
   low pressure (35.9% discount, ₹71,422 revenue).
7. Business is volume-driven, not premium-driven. Mass-market products account for 73.8% of revenue vs. 26.2% for Premium — the growth ceiling on pure volume is a relevant strategic question.
8. Specific loss pockets are identifiable. At the state x category level, the sharpest 2023→2025 declines are Telangana-Grocery Essentials (-33.2%),
   Madhya Pradesh-Beauty & Personal Care (-32.2%), Uttar Pradesh-Beauty & Personal Care (-31.1%), Delhi NCR-Footwear (-29.5%), and Tamil Nadu-Sports & Fitness (-29.0%).

   BUSSINESS INSIGHT
   
   Why growth looks flat but isn't uniformly flat. A single national growth number hides two very different underlying stories: several states are compounding
   solid growth (Kerala, Haryana, Gujarat, Maharashtra) while others are actively shrinking (Tamil Nadu, Odisha, MP). Averaging them together produces a
   misleading "nothing is happening" signal to leadership, when in reality some regions need investment and others need intervention.

   The discounting strategy is optimized for the wrong metric. The data strongly suggests discounting has been used as a volume lever (it does move units,
   +0.39 correlation) without regard to its revenue impact (≈0 correlation). The clearest evidence is the discount-band comparison: revenue per order peaks
   in the 20-40% band and then declines at 60%+, even though units sold keep climbing. This is a classic sign of discounting past the point of diminishing
   returns — deep discounts are compressing final price faster than they're growing basket size.

   Competitive and inventory pressure are triggering reactive discounting that doesn't work. Both "high competition" and "high inventory pressure" conditions
   show the same pattern: discount deepens, but average revenue per order still comes in below the low-pressure baseline. This implies the current playbook
   (discount harder when threatened) is not recovering the revenue it's meant to protect — it may be worth testing bundling, service differentiation, or
   premium-tier upsells instead of price cuts in these conditions.

   Festival timing is a real and differentiated lever. Every category responds positively to Festival events, but the size of the lift varies by 19 percentage
   points across categories (65.4% for Beauty & Personal Care vs. 46.1% for Fashion). Festival calendar planning and inventory allocation could be weighted
   toward the categories that respond most, rather than treated as a blanket sitewide push.

   Category concentration is a growth risk, not just a revenue fact. With Premium Lifestyle and Electronics carrying over half of all revenue, any category-specific
   shock (supply, competition, demand shift) has an outsized effect on the whole business. Diversifying the growth of mid-tier categories (Sports & Fitness,
   Home & Living — both already modestly growing) reduces that concentration risk.


   RECOMMENDATION

1. Reallocate marketing/inventory investment toward proven-growth states (Kerala, Haryana, Gujarat, Maharashtra) rather than spreading spend evenly
   across all 14 states.

2. Open a root-cause review specifically on Tamil Nadu — it is the single steepest decliner (-11.6%) and, combined with its Sports & Fitness drop (-29.0%),
   likely has a state-specific rather than category-specific cause (e.g., local competition, logistics, or stockouts).

3. Cap standard discounting at 40% as a default policy; require a specific business justification (e.g., clearance, dead stock) to go beyond that,
   since the 60%+ band is measurably the least revenue-efficient.

4. Replace reactive high-pressure discounting with non-price levers — bundling, loyalty perks, or premium upsells — for high-competition and high-inventory-pressure
   situations, since deeper discounting in those conditions is not recovering revenue.

5. Weight Festival-period inventory and promotion budget toward Beauty & Personal Care and Electronics, the two highest festival-responders,
   ahead of lower-responding categories like Fashion.

6. Investigate the five worst-declining state-category combinations directly (Telangana-Grocery, MP-Beauty, UP-Beauty, Delhi NCR-Footwear, Tamil Nadu-Sports)
   these are precise enough to action individually rather than folding into a broad category or state strategy.

7. Grow Sports & Fitness and Home & Living deliberately as a portfolio-diversification play, since they are already growing modestly and would reduce
   dependence on the top-2 category concentration.
